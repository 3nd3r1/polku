# Rules and judging

## Eligibility rules (break one and you lose prize eligibility)

- **No code written before the event.** Open-source libraries are fine.
- Nothing built specifically for this idea in advance, and nothing related to an existing company. Either one means disqualification.
- Team of 1–4.
- Participate in at least one challenge. You may submit to **multiple challenges**.
- The whole team must attend live peer review on Sunday (10:30–14:00).
- Pitch video of **2 minutes maximum**, strictly enforced.
- Source code link is required. Organizers must be able to access it.
- **All submissions are MIT-licensed.** Partner APIs stay under partner terms.

## How winners are picked

1. **Challenge winners**: chosen by each partner using their own criteria. **Read each challenge's judging criteria.**
2. **Overall top 5**: peer review by other hackers.
3. **Junction winner**: the top 5 pitch on stage (3 min + 3 min Q&A) and a panel of 7 judges votes.

## General evaluation criteria (peer review)

| Criterion | Question reviewers ask |
|---|---|
| **Innovation** | Is it new or different from the other projects presented? |
| **Impact** | Does it meaningfully help society, and how large a share of the people with the problem does it reach? |
| **Implementation** | Is it realistic, viable and feasible from every angle (tech, business, legal)? |

The theme, "idealism and caution", isn't a formal criterion, but it's a good tiebreaker for the stage judges.

## What this means in practice

- Peer reviewers spend only a few minutes per project at your table, so a **demo that shows its value within 30 seconds** wins.
- Innovation is judged relative to other teams, so avoid the obvious "ChatGPT wrapper for X".
- Impact means you should name who has the problem and how many of them there are.
- Implementation means you should show a plausible business model or adoption path, not just tech.
